The Diamond Sutra

Vajracchedikā Prajñāpāramitā · The Diamond That Cuts Through Illusion

A dialogue between the Buddha and his disciple Subhūti on non-attachment — and the emptiness of every fixed idea, including the idea of a teaching itself. Home to the world's earliest dated printed book (868 CE).

🪷 A cultural & study reference. The full Diamond Sutra is long; below are its key passages with plain-English renderings for study. Wording varies by edition — this is interpretive, not a definitive or liturgical text.
In one minute

What the Diamond Sutra is

The Diamond Sutra (金剛經) is one of the most influential texts in East Asian Buddhism, especially in Chan (Zen). It's structured as a conversation: the elder disciple Subhūti asks the Buddha how a person set on awakening should steady their mind — and the Buddha answers by dismantling, one by one, every fixed thing the mind tries to hold on to.

Its title says it all: like a diamond (the hardest thing, able to cut anything), this wisdom cuts through illusion — including the illusion that there is a solid "self," solid "beings," or even a solid "teaching" to grasp.

Key passages

The heart of the text

Famous lines in original text with English.

應無所住，而生其心。
Yīng wú suǒ zhù, ér shēng qí xīn.
Let the mind arise without resting on anything. Act, care, and give — but without clinging to any of it. (The line said to have awakened the Sixth Patriarch, Huineng.)
凡所有相，皆是虛妄。若見諸相非相，即見如來。
Fán suǒyǒu xiàng, jiē shì xūwàng…
All appearances are illusory. When you see that appearances are not fixed things, you see clearly. Don't mistake the label for the reality.
所謂佛法者，即非佛法。
Suǒwèi fófǎ zhě, jí fēi fófǎ.
What is called "the teaching" is not, in a fixed sense, "the teaching." Even the Dharma is not to be clung to as a solid thing — a raft to cross the river, not a burden to carry.
一切有為法，如夢幻泡影，如露亦如電，應作如是觀。
Yíqiè yǒuwéi fǎ, rú mèng huàn pào yǐng…
All conditioned things are like a dream, an illusion, a bubble, a shadow; like dew, or a flash of lightning — so should you see them. The sutra's famous closing verse on impermanence.
Meaning

What it's really saying

Non-attachment, not indifference

The sutra's core move is "應無所住" — act without clinging. It doesn't ask you to stop caring or stop giving. It asks you to do so freely, without gripping the outcome, the credit, or a fixed story of "me doing this for them."

The emptiness of concepts

Again and again the Buddha names a thing — a self, a being, merit, even the teaching — and then says it is "not" that thing, and so can be called that thing. This isn't wordplay: it's loosening the mind's habit of freezing living reality into fixed labels.

The raft, not the shore

Even the sutra points beyond itself. The teaching is a raft to cross a river — useful, then set down. Clinging to the words as sacred objects misses the point they're trying to make.

Impermanence, gently

It ends on the "dream, bubble, dew, lightning" verse — not as despair, but as an invitation to hold everything a little more lightly, and to be present while it lasts.

Cultural context

On "merit" in this sutra

Merit that clings to nothing 功德

The Diamond Sutra itself talks about merit (gongde) — and then, characteristically, empties the concept: the greatest generosity, it says, is given without attachment to being generous. In this text merit is a cultural teaching device pointing toward selfless action, offered here for reflection — never a guaranteed reward or outcome.
